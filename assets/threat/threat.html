Modern organisations face a relentless, evolving set of adversaries. Understanding who they are, how they operate, and how defenders respond is the foundation of any serious security programme.

01 The Threat Landscape

The term "threat landscape" describes the totality of potential dangers facing an organisation at any given time — actors, their capabilities, their motivations, and the attack techniques they favour. For security practitioners, this isn't abstract: it directly shapes which defences to invest in and which incidents to anticipate.

DFIR — Digital Forensics and Incident Response — is the discipline that sits at the intersection of understanding these threats and responding to them when they materialise. Its three core questions are:

Why? — Understanding who is targeting you and why informs risk models and threat intelligence priorities.

What? — Knowing attacker tactics, cyber threats, and important forensic concepts allows defenders to detect and analyse intrusions accurately.

How? — Essential tools and applications of DFIR turn raw evidence into actionable findings.

A persistent, uncomfortable truth in this field: organisations face a significant skills shortage that equates to higher mean times to detect and respond — a gap attackers actively exploit.

02 Threat Actors & Motivations

Not every attacker is alike. Security teams make fundamentally different decisions depending on who is targeting them. Threat actors are typically grouped by sophistication, resources, and — critically — what they want.

🏛️

Nation-State Actors

Government-sponsored groups with substantial resources. Primary targets are governments and strategic organisations. Objectives include espionage and acquiring sensitive intelligence.

Espionage · Strategic targets
💻

Cybercriminal Groups

Organised criminal enterprises targeting large organisations for financial gain. Ransomware-as-a-Service groups are a dominant subset — they franchise attack infrastructure to affiliates.

Financial gain · Large orgs
🎭

Hacktivists

Ideologically motivated attackers who pursue political or social agendas. Targets tend to be government or corporate entities they oppose. Disruption and reputational damage are common goals.

Political · Social agenda

Key insight: Attacks engineered through social manipulation remain the single biggest security problem for enterprises — because they exploit human factors, not just technical ones. They almost always exploit the basic and known, not the exotic unknown.

04 Initial Access Vectors

How do attackers get in? The entry points are well-documented — and they're almost always exploiting people or misconfigurations, not bleeding-edge zero-days.

The most effective initial access prevention is layered: security awareness training (people), strong MFA and credential hygiene (process), and continuous misconfiguration scanning (technology). No single control is sufficient.

05 Incident Response: The IR 8-18 Framework

When an incident occurs, the response process matters as much as the detection. The IR 8-18 framework defines four phases that structure an effective response — each with distinct goals and stakeholders.

1

Preparation

Improve people through training, establish processes, deploy and configure technology before incidents occur.

2

Detection & Analysis

Detect, alert, escalate, and investigate. Logs are the primary evidence source at this stage.

3

Containment, Eradication & Recovery

Contain the threat, respond, and remediate. Stop further damage; remove attacker presence; restore operations.

4

Post-Incident Activity

Analyse gaps and improve. Every incident is a learning event that should strengthen future defences.

The phases are not strictly linear — detection and containment often run in parallel, and evidence gathered during containment feeds back into analysis. The cyclical nature of good IR is intentional: lessons from phase 4 directly inform a stronger phase 1 next time.

06 Digital Forensics Process

Forensics is the structured, evidence-driven investigation that underpins IR. It answers what happened, when, by whom, and how — in a manner that can withstand legal scrutiny. The data chain is:

Media → Raw Data → Information → Evidence

Each stage involves transformation and validation. Raw bits become structured data; structured data becomes interpreted information; information, once verified and contextualised, becomes admissible evidence.

1

Collection

Identify data sources. Maintain chain of custody and verify integrity with cryptographic hashing at every acquisition step.

2

Examination

Process the collected data and assess it for relevance. Filter noise; surface artefacts of investigative interest.

3

Analysis

Deep analysis of Windows memory and disk artefacts. Establish timelines, identify attacker tools, and reconstruct the intrusion sequence.

4

Reporting

Document findings and recommendations. Reports must be accurate, reproducible, and intelligible to both technical and executive audiences.

07 IR Roles & Responsibilities

Effective incident response requires clear role definition. Two of the most important roles work in parallel — technical depth at the analyst level, strategic coordination at the leadership level.

Security Analyst (IR Persona)
  • Performs all technical investigative work
  • Data collection and preservation
  • Log analysis and correlation
  • Endpoint forensic analysis
  • Malware analysis
  • Threat intelligence and hunting
Security Manager / CISO
  • Co-ordinates teams across the organisation
  • Oversees and prioritises actions
  • Communicates to stakeholders and board
  • Makes business continuity decisions
  • Owns regulatory and legal obligations

The RaaS ecosystem is now a mature criminal economy. Core developers build and maintain ransomware strains and the supporting infrastructure. Access brokers — a specialist role — compromise enterprise environments and sell that initial access to affiliates. Affiliates then deploy the ransomware and take a percentage of the ransom. 09 Key Takeaways

A day studying the threat landscape is dense but essential groundwork. A few things that stand out and are worth carrying forward:

1. Most breaches exploit the known, not the exotic. Phishing, credential stuffing, and misconfigured cloud resources account for the vast majority of initial access events. Solving for the basics covers most of the risk.

2. The skills shortage is a real and present threat multiplier. Higher mean-time-to-detect and mean-time-to-respond create windows of opportunity that attackers exploit systematically. Investing in people and training is a direct security control.

3. Structure your response before an incident happens. Preparation (phase 1 of IR) is not bureaucracy — it is what allows the remaining three phases to function under pressure. Incident response executed without a plan is remediation theatre.

The intersection of forensics, incident response, and threat intelligence is where defenders have the most leverage. Understanding the adversary's playbook — their actors, vectors, and lifecycle — is the prerequisite for every tool, technique, and procedure that follows.
